# AT Deepen: Past the Surface

Most subjects are first seen at the scale they were asked about: a fight, a
plot, a feature, a ticket. What they are *about* sits at other scales, in who
pays, in what happens next, in the odds of the outcome, and in the person
asking. This skill is a set of moves that walk a subject through those
scales, one at a time, until it says something that holds beyond itself.

It is a thought process, not a template. Use the moves that open the subject;
skip those that add nothing. Stop when a move stops revealing something new.

**Use it for** a story, world or character to give weight to; a film, book or
game to understand; an engineering project, migration or product to see
whole; a decision, an institution, a moment in history.

**Do not use it for** a question with a decidable answer (a bug, a number, a
fact), or when the user wants a quick reply. Depth no one asked for is noise.

---

## The moves

### 1. Mechanism: how does it produce its outcome?
Ask what makes the result happen, not what the result is. Build or trace the
model, and let the outcome come out of it. A result that is asserted, scripted
or tuned to look right says nothing. One that falls out of a faithful mechanism
is evidence.
- Story: why does this character fail? What in their situation makes it likely?
- Engineering: why is the system shaped like this? What forces bent it?

### 2. Ledger: who pays, and who gains?
Every system moves cost from someone to someone else. Find the people who
carry the cost but are not in the frame: the soldier's family, the on-call
engineer, the user who reads the error, the town downstream. Then find who
collects. Most of what a subject is about lives in this gap.

### 3. Scales: zoom out and back in
Walk the subject through its scales, and ask at each what is true here that
was invisible at the last: the body, the person, the group, the institution,
the era, the species. Or in engineering: the function, the service, the team,
the company, the industry. Then come back to the smallest true detail. One
mouldy ration carries more than a paragraph of history.

### 4. Time: before and after
Run it backwards: what made this, and what was it made to replace? Run it
forwards: second-order effects, what the children inherit, what replaces it.
Follow the consequences past the point where the story usually stops.

### 5. Odds: the lives you don't hear about
Ask how likely this outcome was, and who did not make it into the record.
Survivorship hides most of the truth. Put a number on it when you can,
1 in 256, a base rate, a failure rate, and state how rough it is. The person
who succeeded is often the lucky one, not the best one; say which.

### 6. The other edge
Every tool, institution and side cuts both ways. Find the cost of the hero's
side and the reason of the villain's. Distrust a one-sided frame, including
the one you started with. Technology that saves also kills; the system that
oppresses also feeds.

### 7. Mirror: why this subject, for this person?
Ask what draws the creator or the asker to it. Their own smaller experience
of the same shape often gives the work its weight, and naming it makes the
work truer. Then say where the parallel breaks: real lives usually have exits
the story's character did not. Be careful and kind here; offer it as a reading,
and only go into private material the person has shared or invited.

### 8. Distil: what transfers?
Ask what the subject teaches outside itself. A lineage that survives three
centuries teaches about risk spread and portable skills; a failed migration
teaches about who held the knowledge. State it plainly, as something a person
could use.

---

## How to run it

1. **Name the surface.** One line: what it appears to be.
2. **Pick the moves that open it.** Usually three to five. Mechanism and Ledger
   almost always; Mirror only when the person invites it.
3. **Go one move at a time**, each building on the last, and show the step:
   the reader should see how each layer was reached, not just the conclusion.
4. **Ground every claim.** Verify facts, dates and figures; mark estimates as
   estimates and speculation as speculation. Never invent history to make a
   point.
5. **End with the distillation** and the one concrete detail that carries it.
6. **Offer, don't impose.** Depth is a gift to the work, not a lecture. No
   moralising; let the mechanism and the detail make the case.

## Guard rails

- **Depth is not darkness.** Find the slightly beautiful thing too; the weight
  of a story comes from what is lost, which means something was worth having.
- **No unasked psychoanalysis.** The Mirror move needs an invitation.
- **Stop when it stops adding.** A ninth layer that repeats the eighth is noise.
- **Keep the user's frame primary.** Deepen their subject, don't replace it with yours.

## Example (compressed)

*Surface:* a physics fighting game where a Ming foot soldier fights cavalry.
*Mechanism:* his low kill rate comes out of the simulation (armour, reach, fatigue), not a script.
*Ledger:* his merit becomes his officer's promotion; his home pays in labour and debt.
*Scales:* the line, the family, the court, the dynasty, the lineage to today.
*Odds:* surviving eight battles at about even odds each is 1 in 256.
*Other edge:* the steppe had its own levies and its own spent men.
*Mirror:* the creator's own smaller experience of being spent by an institution gives the story weight; their real life, unlike his, has exits.
*Distil:* lineages survive by keeping children alive, spreading risk across places, and carrying portable skills, not by fighting.
*Detail:* half a cake of mouldy ration and a letter with his name on it.
